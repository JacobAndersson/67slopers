# Measure the wall with gravity, not depth

Rivet should estimate wall angle mainly from the phone's own gravity sensor, and use the camera only where geometry it already knows can do the work. Laid flat against the board, a phone reads tilt to about **±1°**: static clinical validations measure a 0.7–1.1° mean difference against motion capture and a 95% minimum detectable change of 1.8° ([PubMed 33710269](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33710269/)). That method works on iOS, Android and web, needs no model and adds nothing to startup. For a single photo, the reliable route is IMU gravity at the shutter plus the geometry of a known rectangle (the MoonBoard's frame or hold grid), which plausibly gives 1–3°. Monocular depth models such as MoGe-2 or Depth Anything are a weaker fallback. Their per-pixel normal errors average **9–20°** on standard benchmarks ([Metric3D v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.15506v4)), and nobody has published a whole-plane error, so the ±2–3° target is unproven for them. For holds, the same rule applies. On MoonBoards the app already knows every hold position, so a four-corner homography onto `src/lib/moonboard/boards.ts` replaces detection entirely. On arbitrary walls the field has no permissively licensed pretrained hold model: nearly every open one is Ultralytics YOLO under AGPL-3.0. The workable plan is tap-to-segment first and a fine-tuned Apache-2.0 segmenter later, run through react-native-executorch with models bundled in the binary and loaded only on the feature screen.

## Every image method still needs the accelerometer

Overhang is the angle between the wall plane and gravity, and gravity is not in the pixels. Depth maps, normal maps, vanishing points and homographies all give the wall's orientation **in the camera frame**. Turning that into "40° past vertical" needs the phone's gravity vector at capture time (iOS `CMDeviceMotion.gravity`, Android `TYPE_GRAVITY`, Expo `DeviceMotion`), mapped into camera axes for the photo's orientation. This sets the shape of the whole feature. The IMU is mandatory in every mode, the error budget is vision error plus a small static tilt error, and the gravity sample must be taken while the phone is still. Static phone tilt is good: a phone's built-in inclinometer is quoted at one-degree precision ([PMC9130239](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9130239/)), and under 2° of variability is attributable to phone, app and examiner together ([Touro](https://touroscholar.touro.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1049&context=chhs_pubs)). Dynamic gravity estimates while walking are worse, about 5° on average ([RGNet](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/357038307_RGNet_Robust_Gravity_Estimation_Neural_Network_for_IMU-based_Localization_Using_Smartphone)). That is why capture should wait for a stable reading rather than fire on tap.

The market confirms the simple route. MoonBoard, Kilter and Tension apps all have the user *select* the angle; none measures it ([climbingfacts](https://climbingfacts.com/how-to-use-the-moonboard/), [climbing.com](https://www.climbing.com/gear/6-best-climbing-apps/), [Gripped](https://gripped.com/indoor-climbing/how-to-use-a-tension-board/)). The one dedicated tool found, Climb Angle Meter, tells you to "place the back of your phone against the wall". It calibrates for the camera bump and reports degrees past vertical, which generic clinometers get wrong because they measure from horizontal ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.climbangletool.climbangletool&hl=en_US)). No camera-based wall-angle app was found. Commercial board angles are also discrete (MoonBoard 25°/40°; Kilter 0–70° in 5° steps), so on those boards any method only has to be right to within half a step and then snap. A continuous reading matters only on home spray walls and gym walls.

### Three angle methods compared

| Method | Expected error | What it needs | Fit with Rivet's rules |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inclinometer (phone on board, flip to calibrate) | ~±1° (static clinical data, MDC95 1.8°) | expo-sensors only | No model, no launch cost, works on web |
| Photo: IMU + known rectangle (homography or vanishing points) | plausibly 1–3°, unmeasured on walls | expo-camera (already used), EXIF or self-calibrated focal, pure-TS math | No model; testable in `src/lib` |
| Photo: metric depth/normal model + RANSAC plane | guessed 3–8°, no published whole-plane figure | 35–100 MB model, ML runtime | Optional, lazily loaded, labelled approximate |
| ARKit/ARCore live session | likely ~1° with LiDAR, unquantified | heavy native AR stack | Poor; no slanted planes on phones |

The homography route deserves the attention. Fitting the four corners of the board or grid to a rectangle of known aspect ratio and decomposing it with the camera intrinsics gives the plane's rotation; the two in-plane vanishing directions (grid rows and columns) give the same normal through their cross product. The literature on vanishing points with an IMU prior reports **0.83–2.54°** camera-rotation error, robust even to a 3.9° gravity prior error ([Pautrat et al., ICCV 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.10694)), and gravity-constrained vanishing-point detection ran at 15 fps on an iPhone 5S with under 2° orientation error ([Pattern Recognition Letters 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167865518302800)). A note of caution: a classic patent derives plane normals from a vanishing point crossed with gravity, which assumes upright faces ([US 9098229](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9098229)). That is exactly the assumption an overhang breaks, so the normal must come from the wall's own two in-plane directions. The method's weaknesses can all be handled. A face-on shot is poorly conditioned for tilt, so the UI should ask for a photo 20–40° off-axis. Lens distortion should be undone first. Corner error falls if the user drags the corners. A missing focal length can be self-calibrated from the known aspect ratio. Nobody has measured this pipeline on real climbing walls, and MoonBoard's physical panel dimensions still need confirming from Moon's spec before the aspect ratio can be trusted.

The AR frameworks look like a shortcut and are not one. An Apple engineer states that "ARKit only detects planes that are either perpendicular or parallel to gravity" ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/723532)). Slanted plane alignment arrived only on visionOS 2 ([WWDC24](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10100/)), and ARCore likewise classifies horizontal and vertical planes ([ARCore fundamentals](https://developers.google.com/ar/develop/fundamentals)). The workaround is to fit your own plane to scene depth or ARCore Raw Depth, which ships per-pixel confidence ([ARCore Raw Depth](https://developers.google.com/ar/develop/java/depth/raw-depth?hl=en)). But it needs a live AR session and a heavy native dependency, which is out of proportion for a one-off reading.

## Depth models can find the plane but cannot yet promise the angle

If Rivet adds a model-based fallback for featureless spray walls, the model family matters more than its size. **Relative (affine-invariant) depth does not preserve plane angles.** LeReS showed that an unknown depth shift and an unknown focal length both distort recovered 3D shape, and that its own module for recovering them "can fail" ([Yin et al., CVPR 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.09365)). That rules out the convenient default. Depth Anything V2 Small (24.8M params, Apache-2.0) and DA3 Small are both shipped by react-native-executorch, and both output *relative* depth ([executorch depth docs](https://docs.swmansion.com/react-native-executorch/docs/extensions/depth-estimation)). The right family outputs metric point maps with intrinsics, or normals directly. Within it, **MoGe-2 ViT-S-normal** fits an app best. It has 35M parameters and gives a metric point map, normals and camera intrinsics in one forward pass. Its code is MIT ([microsoft/MoGe](https://github.com/microsoft/moge)); the weights are reported as MIT only by a third party ([LibreYOLO](https://www.libreyolo.com/docs/models/moge-2)), so verify the HF cards before shipping. Several strong alternatives are excluded for a distributed app by their licences: UniDepthV2 is CC BY-NC 4.0 ([UniDepth](https://github.com/lpiccinelli-eth/UniDepth)), Depth Pro 2 is under Apple's research-only licence ([HF apple/DepthPro2-H](https://huggingface.co/apple/DepthPro2-H)), and Depth Anything V2 Base/Large are CC BY-NC 4.0 ([Depth-Anything-V2](https://github.com/DepthAnything/Depth-Anything-V2)).

How accurate this route is remains the open question. Metric3D v2's normals have mean/median angular errors of **13.1°/7.1°** zero-shot on NYUv2 and 9.2°/5.0° on ScanNet ([Metric3D v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.15506v4)). Normal-only models sit around 15–19° mean: DSINE 16.4° and Lotus-D 16.2° on NYUv2 ([PINE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29389)). Those are per-pixel figures, dominated by edges and clutter. A wall is one large plane, and a RANSAC fit over thousands of back-projected points averages most of the random error away; holds stand only centimetres proud and fall outside a tight inlier threshold. What survives the averaging is *systematic* bias, chiefly a wrong field of view, which skews the fitted tilt on oblique shots, and the model bending surfaces it finds unusual. Feeding in known intrinsics (EXIF focal length and sensor size) removes the first; MoGe-2, Metric3D and DSINE all accept them, and DSINE assumes a 60° FOV without them ([DSINE](https://github.com/baegwangbin/DSINE)). Volumes are the other real hazard: they are large planar outliers that can capture the fit, so keep the largest inlier set inside a wall mask. The resulting **3–8°** whole-plane estimate is this report's inference, not a measured number. The depth mode should therefore show a confidence and say "approximate". Fortunately it is the cheapest mode to validate: photograph a MoonBoard at its known 40° from a dozen positions and compare.

Speed is not the constraint. Apple's Core ML Depth Anything V2 Small runs in **31–34 ms** on iPhone 12 Pro Max and 15 Pro Max at fp16, at 49.8 MB ([HF apple](https://huggingface.co/apple/coreml-depth-anything-v2-small)). Qualcomm reports 21 ms on Snapdragon 8 Elite and 67 ms on 8 Gen 1 through the NPU, with peak memory up to about 376 MB ([Qualcomm AI Hub](https://huggingface.co/qualcomm/Depth-Anything-V2)). Expect a mid-range phone on a GPU or CPU delegate to be several times slower, perhaps 100–500 ms (unmeasured), which is still fine for one photo. No phone latency was found for MoGe-2 ViT-S itself; similar ViT-S size suggests the same order.

## Known boards need registration, arbitrary walls need a licence decision

Hold segmentation splits cleanly into two problems. For MoonBoards, holds sit at fixed t-nut positions on an A–K by 1–18 grid, with LEDs at each position lighting green for start, blue for intermediate and red for finish ([MoonBoard guide](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5b680ff22714e5a2859144bf/t/5e4422a70c7e7311c6a60ecc/1581523625176/How+to+use+The+MoonBoard.pdf)). Rivet already stores every layout and hold set in `src/lib/moonboard/boards.ts`, so detection is redundant. Four corners, tapped or found by quad detection, give a homography. Projecting the known grid gives every hold's pixel position, and sampling hue and brightness there reads which LEDs are lit. This is pure TypeScript, needs no data or licence, and the same homography also feeds the photo-angle mode. No open-source version of this exists; chessboard detection is the closest documented technique ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chessboard_detection)). Its accuracy on phone photos, and how LED colours survive gym lighting and auto-exposure saturation, are both unmeasured.

Arbitrary walls are where the trouble lies, and the trouble is legal before it is technical. Almost every open hold project uses Ultralytics YOLO and is AGPL-3.0. Examples are climb-beta's YOLO11-seg ([repo](https://github.com/aakash-tir/climb-beta)) and ClimbColor, an on-device iPhone app shipping a YOLO26-seg Core ML model at 640 px ([repo](https://github.com/seesaw-earth/ClimbColor)). Ultralytics says AGPL covers "the training code and the models produced by that training code" ([LibreYOLO licence guide](https://www.libreyolo.com/articles/yolo-licenses-explained)); a closed app needs its Enterprise Licence ([Ultralytics](https://www.ultralytics.com/license)). Rivet is free, so AGPL does not forbid shipping, but it would oblige releasing the whole app's source under AGPL-compatible terms. The repo's `LICENSE` is currently the Expo template's MIT text, so that would be a deliberate project-level change. If Rivet takes it, YOLO26n-seg at **2.7M params** is the smallest proven on-device hold segmenter ([rf-detr comparison](https://github.com/roboflow/rf-detr)), and YOLO11n-seg runs in 2.4–9.4 ms on Snapdragon NPUs ([Qualcomm](https://huggingface.co/qualcomm/YOLOv11-Segmentation)). If not, the permissive options are RF-DETR-Seg, which is Apache-2.0 at every segmentation size but 33.6M params even at Nano (mask AP 40.3 on COCO) ([rf-detr](https://github.com/roboflow/rf-detr)), and RTMDet-Ins, YOLOX, D-FINE, DEIM and RT-DETR, all Apache-2.0 ([LibreYOLO](https://www.libreyolo.com/articles/yolo-licenses-explained)). Avoid YOLO-NAS: its weights are non-commercial and the download URLs are dying ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/licensing-and-usage-terms-for-yolo-nas-model/326342)). No permissively licensed *pretrained* hold weights exist, so a fine-tune is unavoidable on that path.

The data is thin and mostly boxes. The cleanest training source is Roboflow's "Climbing Holds and Volumes": about 1,650–1,717 indoor images and 60,804 boxes in two classes, CC BY 4.0, with a 13.9:1 hold-to-volume imbalance ([climbml](https://github.com/kianjfeiz/climbml)). Its several same-named copies need a person to check in a browser for masks and licence ([climb-beta #12](https://github.com/aakash-tir/climb-beta/issues/12)). The largest mask set, ClimbInst, has 89,020 SAM 3-generated, hand-corrected masks over about 1.2–1.5k images, but it names no licence and inherits mixed source terms ([HF ClimbInst](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ClimbVision/ClimbInst), [1SS28 audit](https://github.com/1SS28/climbing-beta)). The only clearly licensed academic mask set, Sláma's, is CC BY-SA 4.0 and has fewer than 20 labelled images ([Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/tomasslama/indoor-climbing-gym-hold-segmentation)). The practical recipe follows from this. Train on CC BY 4.0 boxes, generate masks with SAM 2 (Apache-2.0) or SAM 3 (Meta's commercial-friendly SAM License, [Roboflow](https://roboflow.com/model-licenses/segment-anything-3)) as a teacher, as ClimbInst and CruxCoach did ([CruxCoach PR #49](https://github.com/CruxCoach/CruxCoach/pull/49)), and keep ClimbInst and Sláma for internal evaluation only. Published accuracy is nearly absent. A YOLOv5 box model scored mAP50 0.81 on egocentric frames ([Sensors 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11479384/)), and a second-hand mask mAP50 of 91.3% is quoted for the climbtag set ([Boulderscanner](https://github.com/TimHarri/DAV_OG_Boulderscanner)). Budget for building your own test set.

The same failure modes recur across projects. Small footholds get missed. Touching holds and chips on volumes merge. Chalk passes for white and white holds get discarded as chalk. Glare clips pixels. Pink and red get confused. Wall seams and two-tone holds create false edges ([Spricc](https://github.com/mmarci778/Spricc), [climb-beta](https://github.com/aakash-tir/climb-beta)). Classical colour thresholding and k-means fail on these walls, and edges plus watershed take about 8 s per wall on one core while still over-splitting ([Spricc](https://github.com/mmarci778/Spricc)).

## Tap first, group by colour, detect automatically last

Every working project separates "is this a hold" from "what colour is it". The detector is trained with heavy hue augmentation because "colour is not what makes something a hold" ([Spricc](https://github.com/mmarci778/Spricc)). Colour is then computed over each mask. ClimbColor's grouping is the one to copy. It matches in CIELAB with CIEDE2000 at **ΔE00 ≤ 10** (adjustable) and weights lightness at half (kL = 2) so uneven light keeps a route together ([ClimbColor](https://github.com/seesaw-earth/ClimbColor)). Add climb-beta's tricks: grey-world white balance on wall pixels, chalk rejection by low saturation and high value, pink-versus-red by saturation and lightness rather than hue, and a separate `tape` class for tape-marked routes ([climb-beta](https://github.com/aakash-tir/climb-beta)). The UX should be "tap one hold, highlight every hold within ΔE00 of it". Global clustering breaks because hold colours run in a continuum from orange through yellow to pink, which defeated single-linkage clustering in one project ([1SS28](https://github.com/1SS28/climbing-beta)). Tapping sidesteps the problem and gives colour-blind users a reliable path.

Tap-to-segment is also the right first step for masks. MobileSAM and EfficientSAM run at about **4.9 FPS** on an iPhone 14, so roughly 200 ms to encode a photo once, after which each tap runs only the tiny decoder ([EdgeSAM paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.06660v2)). EdgeSAM is far faster (38.7 FPS, about 26 ms encode), but its NTU S-Lab licence is likely non-commercial and must be read before use ([EdgeSAM GitHub](https://github.com/chongzhou96/EdgeSAM)). The MobileSAM and EfficientSAM licences are believed Apache-2.0 but were not verified from primary sources. Two zero-weight or bundled alternatives exist. On iOS 17+, Apple Vision's `VNGenerateForegroundInstanceMaskRequest` gives class-agnostic instance masks with `instanceAtPoint` for taps and downloads nothing ([WWDC23 notes](https://wwdcnotes.com/documentation/wwdc23-10176-lift-subjects-from-images-in-your-app/)), though subject lifting is tuned for foreground objects and is untested on hold-dense walls. MediaPipe's MagicTouch interactive segmenter runs cross-platform at 67–130 ms on a Pixel 6, but the licence of its v2 model is unclear ([HF litert-community](https://huggingface.co/litert-community/MediaPipe-InteractiveSegmenter-MagicTouch), [issue #6400](https://github.com/google-ai-edge/mediapipe/issues/6400)). ML Kit subject segmentation is out: its model arrives through Google Play services and has no bundled form ([ML Kit docs](https://developers.google.com/ml-kit/vision/subject-segmentation/android)), which breaks the no-network rule.

## executorch with bundled models is the runtime to prototype

**react-native-executorch** is the only React Native library covering depth and instance segmentation with tested pre- and post-processing. It requires the New Architecture, Expo SDK 55+ dev builds, iOS 17+ and Android minSdk 26, and it pins react-native-worklets to `>=0.10.0 <0.14.0` ([README](https://github.com/software-mansion/react-native-executorch)). Rivet's current worklets 0.10.1 sits inside that range. Since v0.8 it exposes `useInstanceSegmentation` with YOLO and RF-DETR models returning per-instance binary `Uint8Array` masks ([SWM blog](https://swmansion.com/blog/react-native-executorch-v0.8.0-a-library-milestone/)). Depth estimation was merged on 20 October 2026, with XNNPACK, Core ML and Vulkan backends and custom `.pte` loading ([PR #1552](https://github.com/software-mansion/react-native-executorch/pull/1552)), so expect churn. It has no SAM hook, so tap-to-segment would mean exporting a MobileSAM `.pte` yourself. There is one offline trap. Its resource fetcher sends a HEAD request for every remote source, with no timeout, so pass only bundled `require()` assets or `file://` paths, as Software Mansion's own Private Mind app had to do ([private-mind PR #447](https://github.com/software-mansion-labs/private-mind/pull/447)). Whether `initExecutorch()` can be deferred from the entry point to the feature screen is unverified. It must be, under the instant-startup rule. The fallback is **react-native-fast-tflite** (MIT, Nitro, Expo config plugin, Core ML and GPU delegates). It is a bare interpreter, so NMS, mask decoding and depth normalisation would be hand-written ([fast-tflite](https://github.com/mrousavy/react-native-fast-tflite)). Avoid onnxruntime-react-native: on the New Architecture it throws at module load unless its JSI API is installed first, and that is still unresolved ([dev.to field report](https://dev.to/vdrosatos/phone-first-cloud-when-unsure-on-device-siglip-2-vs-gemini-on-3170-waste-photos-1kkd)).

Delivery is simple at this scale. Bundle the models as plain assets: an fp16 depth model of about 25–50 MB plus a few MB for a nano segmenter keeps the app far below Apple's 200 MB cellular prompt and Play's 500 MB base module ([Play size limits](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9859372)). Play Asset Delivery is served by Google Play ([PAD docs](https://developer.android.com/guide/playcore/asset-delivery)), so it would fail for the sideloaded APK `npm run build:android` produces. Use a still photo from the existing expo-camera instead of VisionCamera v5 frame processors. Draw mask outlines with the app's react-native-svg and leave per-pixel heatmaps for later. On web, Transformers.js can run Depth Anything offline once `env.allowRemoteModels = false`, `env.allowLocalModels = true` and local WASM paths are set before any pipeline is created ([transformers.js env](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers.js/api/env)). Its defaults fetch from the HF Hub and a CDN.

### Phased plan

| Phase | Ships | New dependencies | Exit check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Inclinometer | "Measure angle": phone on board, flip-to-calibrate, wait for a stable reading, snap to the board's presets, store on the setup | `expo-sensors` | ±1° against a digital level; works on web via DeviceMotion |
| 2. Board registration | Photo of MoonBoard → drag four corners → homography in pure TS (`src/lib/vision/`) → projected grid overlay, lit-LED read-out, and the photo angle from homography + gravity at the shutter + EXIF focal | none beyond expo-camera | Photo angle within 2° of the known 25°/40° from 10+ positions; LED read matches the problem shown on the lights |
| 3. Tap-to-select holds | Spray-wall photo → tap a hold → MobileSAM mask (or Apple Vision on iOS) → ΔE00 grouping with grey-world WB and chalk rejection → save as an own problem | react-native-executorch (lazy), bundled MobileSAM `.pte` | Licence of MobileSAM verified; encode < 1 s on a mid-range Android; nothing loaded before the screen opens |
| 4. Depth fallback | Angle for walls with no rectangle: MoGe-2 ViT-S-normal + EXIF intrinsics + RANSAC in a wall mask, shown as "approximate" with a confidence | bundled MoGe-2 `.pte` (~35–70 MB) | Measured whole-plane error on an in-house set; hide the mode if > 3° |
| 5. Automatic hold detection | Whole-wall detection with long-press re-detect on a crop for footholds | fine-tuned RF-DETR-Seg-N or RTMDet-Ins-tiny (Apache-2.0) trained on CC BY 4.0 data with SAM 2/3 masks; credits screen | Recall ≥ 95% at mask IoU 0.5 on a held-out set; phone latency measured |

Phases 1 and 2 deliver most of the value. They add no model weights and nothing to the launch path, and they keep all the logic pure and testable in `src/lib`, in line with the project's rule that screens only render. Phases 3–5 each add an asset and a runtime behind a lazily loaded route, so each should be gated on a measurement rather than on the literature.

## Conclusion

The research shifts the problem. Wall angle is not a vision problem that needs a better model; it is a gravity problem in which vision only has to supply the wall's orientation relative to the camera. A known rectangle does that more precisely than any monocular network can show on paper today. Hold segmentation is similar. Rivet's own data, the MoonBoard layouts, already does the hardest part on the boards it supports. The real decision for arbitrary walls is licensing, not accuracy: shipping AGPL YOLO is the fastest route to a proven on-device segmenter, and declining it costs a fine-tune on a permissive architecture that is ten times larger.

Two gaps decide whether the later phases are worth building, and both can be closed in-house within days. Nobody has measured whole-plane angle error for depth models, or homography-plus-IMU error, on real climbing walls. Nobody has published hold-mask recall on a shared benchmark. A small test set of MoonBoard photos at known angles, plus a few hundred hand-checked spray-wall masks, would be the most valuable research artefact Rivet could produce. It would turn every "plausibly" in this report into a number before any UI promises one.
